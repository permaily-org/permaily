## Procedures (operator)

- **Invitations and sanctions.** The operator is a US person, so OFAC's sanctions bind every invitation, wherever the person lives ([FAQ 11](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/1501)). Before sending one:
  1. Ask the person's full name, the country and region where they live, and that they are 18 or over.
  2. Search the name in OFAC's [Sanctions List Search](https://sanctionssearch.ofac.treas.gov/), which covers the SDN List and OFAC's other lists. A match that cannot be cleared means no invitation.
  3. Ask whether they act for a government or an organisation. Anyone acting for a blocked person, or for a government whose property is blocked (on 2026-11-12, FAQ 10 names the governments of Cuba, Iran, North Korea and Venezuela), gets no invitation; the service is for people's own mail anyway.
  4. Check where they live against the places OFAC FAQ 10 names as under comprehensive sanctions on that day. On 2026-11-12: Cuba, Iran, North Korea, the Crimea region of Ukraine, and the so-called Donetsk and Luhansk People's Republic regions. North Korea: no invitation. The others: general licences authorise services incident to the exchange of communications over the internet, such as email, for private people there, but not for blocked persons or the government (see "Sanctions" below), and Microsoft's API Terms forbid access "from an embargoed country". Until the terms of AWS, OpenAI and the sign-in providers for those places are checked and the general licence relied on is named in the record, invite nobody who lives in one of them.
  5. Record the date, the list searched and its result, the place, and the general licence relied on, if any, and keep the record 10 years ([31 CFR 501.601](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/501.601)).

  If the operator later learns that a person is on a list, acts for a sanctioned government, or lives in one of those places without the checks above, block the account and find out whether anything must be reported to OFAC. A block by IP address or by country is not compliance, and none is claimed.
- **Inviting someone in Europe.** Before inviting someone in the EU, the EEA, Switzerland or the UK, go through "Before inviting anyone in Europe". Inviting before it is met is the operator's choice and a risk taken knowingly.
- **Requests from authorities.** Check that the request is valid and is the right kind for what it asks (below). Give only what it covers. Tell the person unless a court order or a statute forbids it, or as soon as it no longer does. Keep a record of the request and the answer. Never promise a way to decrypt sealed data; it does not exist.
- **Preservation.** On a request under 2703(f), preserve the named records for 90 days, and 90 more if renewed, using item 4; without it, by stopping the removal of that account by hand and noting it. Delete what was preserved when the time ends unless process arrives.
- **Child sexual abuse material.** On actual knowledge, report to NCMEC's CyberTipline as soon as reasonably possible, and preserve what was reported for one year (18 U.S.C. 2258A(a), (h)). No monitoring or scanning is required (2258A(f)), and none is done; the operator cannot read mail.
- **Incidents.** Write down when a breach was determined. Florida: individuals within 30 days; the Department of Legal Affairs within 30 days if 500 or more Florida residents; credit reporting agencies if more than 1,000 at once (see "Florida"). Other states where affected people live may set other rules: check each. Microsoft: a breach involving data from its APIs, or a vulnerability in the connection to them, goes to secure@microsoft.com, with no public statement about the Microsoft APIs without its permission (API Terms section 4). A notice by mail to every affected person is sent by hand until there is tooling.
- **Changes to the terms.** Bump `TERMS_VERSION` and `LEGAL_UPDATED`; for a material change, mail every account at least 30 days before (by hand until there is tooling) and, once item 1 is built, ask for acceptance again.
- **Shutting down.** Mail every account as early as possible, aiming at 30 days, then remove accounts as a deletion would.

## No postal address

What is given up, or must not be done, while no postal address is published:

- **DMCA safe harbour (17 U.S.C. 512(c)).** It needs a designated agent whose "name, address, phone number, and electronic mail address" are on the website and filed with the Copyright Office (512(c)(2)); the agent's address may be a P.O. box but the service provider's own street address must be filed (37 C.F.R. 201.38(b)(1), (b)(4)); the designation expires after three years. It also needs a repeat-infringer policy "adopted and reasonably implemented" and told to account holders (512(i)(1)(A)). None exists, so the safe harbour is given up for now. The exposure is small while nothing a person stores is public, and grows if the service ever publishes what people store.
- **Promotional mail.** CAN-SPAM requires a valid physical postal address in commercial messages; the FTC says a message that is only transactional or relationship content, such as one that "notifies the recipient about a change in the terms or features of a ... account", "may not contain false or misleading routing information, but is otherwise exempt from most provisions". The service sends only sign-in codes and notices about the account: keep anything promotional out of them, and send no newsletters until there is an address.
- **Microsoft publisher verification** needs a verified Partner Program account of an organisation; basic sign-in works without it.

## Before inviting anyone in Europe

Conditions to meet first, not things done. "Europe" means the EU, the EEA, Switzerland and the UK. The terms do not exclude Europe: whether and when someone there is invited is the operator's choice at that moment. Inviting someone there before these conditions are met is a risk the operator takes knowingly, listed in "Residual risks". The privacy page's "If you live in Europe" says what is not in place; update it as each item here is done.

- **Territorial scope assessed properly.** The GDPR reaches a controller outside the EU for "the offering of goods or services, irrespective of whether a payment of the data subject is required" to people in the Union (Art. 3(2)(a)); an invitation to someone there can be that offer by itself, and a person's self-declared residence is not an assessment. Do one for each country invited to. Sources for the GDPR here: [gdpr-info.eu](https://gdpr-info.eu/) (EUR-Lex served no text to automated reads on 2026-11-12).
- **A representative** in the EU, named in writing (Art. 27(1)), unless the processing "is occasional, does not include, on a large scale, processing of special categories of data" and is unlikely to be a risk (27(2)(a)); a mail service's processing is not occasional. One in the UK too, under the UK GDPR's own Art. 27, while the operator has no establishment there; Switzerland's rule for a representative is to be checked. None is named today, and the privacy page says so.
- **Roles.** The household exemption, processing "by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity" (Art. 2(2)(c)), does not by itself make Permaily the controller of every operation on forwarded mail, and a natural person using it for freelance work may be a controller with Permaily as processor. Decide the roles per operation, and offer an Art. 28 agreement where Permaily is a processor.
- **Lawful bases with assessments**: contract for the account and the service; a written legitimate-interests assessment for security and for other people's data in mail; an Art. 9(2) condition for special categories in mail, which may not exist for senders' data (no regulator's answer was found).
- **Cookies.** The Article 29 Working Party said "persistent login cookies which store an authentication token across browser sessions are not exempted", and suggested a "remember me (uses cookies)" checkbox ([Opinion 04/2012, WP194](https://ec.europa.eu/justice/article-29/documentation/opinion-recommendation/files/2012/wp194_en.pdf), section 3.2): the session cookie lasts 30 days in the browser and needs either consent, a "keep me signed in" choice, or a reasoned national exemption for each country; the provider cookie lasts up to 20 minutes, not 10. The first draft's conclusion that no banner is needed did not hold and is withdrawn.
- **Notice.** Art. 13 in full (portability, which data is required and which optional, transfer safeguards), and Art. 14 for senders whose data arrives in mail. Art. 11(2) lifts only "Articles 15 to 20" where the controller cannot identify the person, so it does not excuse Art. 14.
- **Transfers.** Chapter V analysis by flow (person to service, service to AWS, service to OpenAI, service to connections), not by where the operator is.
- **Records and DPIA.** A full Art. 30 record ([data.md](data.md) is a data inventory, not one) and a complete DPIA before the processing starts; mail, and a model deciding by meaning, are the kind of processing the regulators' DPIA criteria name, to be checked against their guidance (not reopened here).
- **Consumer law.** The first draft said the Digital Content Directive (2019/770) does not apply because the service is free; that shortcut is withdrawn. The Directive also covers services supplied in exchange for personal data, with an exception for data processed only to supply the service or meet legal requirements; whether Permaily's security processing and admin log stay inside it needs an analysis against the text, which could not be opened from here on 2026-11-12. The terms also need review against the EU's unfair-terms and consumer-jurisdiction rules and the UK's, before anyone there is invited.
- **OpenAI**: its data processing addendum and its EU and UK transfer terms checked for the flow of mail to it. Mail goes to OpenAI before zero data retention is approved (decided above), so this is about the terms of that flow, not about waiting for it.
- **Breaches**: "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours" to the supervisory authority (Art. 33(1)).

## What was researched

### Who must comply with what (United States)

- **Stored Communications Act.** Permaily holds mail for people and will disclose parts of it, so 18 U.S.C. 2702 applies whether it is an "electronic communication service" (2510(15)) or a "remote computing service", "the provision to the public of computer storage or processing services" (2711(2)); which one changes details, and probably both describe parts of it. Per kind of disclosure:
  - *To a connection the person approves, and to OpenAI for a rule about meaning:* content, so it needs an exception of 2702(b). The fitting one is "the lawful consent of the originator or an addressee or intended recipient of such communication, or the subscriber in the case of remote computing service" (b)(3); the person forwarding is the addressee. (b)(5), "necessarily incident to the rendition of the service", may also cover a model the person turned on, but consent is stronger. The terms (section 5) state the consent, and engineering item 2 records it specifically. Fairly sure.
  - *To AWS (storage, SES) and Cloudflare (DNS):* AWS stores only sealed content; SES carries Permaily's own messages. (b)(4) and (b)(5) cover these. Fairly sure.
  - *To the government, content:* a warrant, or for a remote computing service a subpoena or a 2703(d) court order with prior notice to the customer (2703(a), (b)); the operator can produce only ciphertext.
  - *To the government, records:* basic subscriber information (name, address, session times and durations, length of service, means of payment) by subpoena (2703(c)(2)); other records by a 2703(d) order, a warrant, or the subscriber's consent (c)(1). A 2703(d) order needs "specific and articulable facts". Disclosure to non-government parties of records is allowed (2702(c)(6)), but the privacy page promises nothing of the kind.
  - *Emergencies:* content or records to a government entity when the provider believes in good faith that "an emergency involving danger of death or serious physical injury" requires it (2702(b)(8), (c)(4)).
  - *Preservation:* on a government request, preserve records "for a period of 90 days, which shall be extended for an additional 90-day period" on renewal (2703(f)). This overrides deletion for the records named; the privacy page and the terms say so.
  - Sources: [2702](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2702), [2703](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2703), [2711](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2711).
- **18 U.S.C. 2258A.** A provider with "actual knowledge" of apparent child sexual abuse material must report it to NCMEC as soon as reasonably possible ((a)(1)); a report is a request to preserve the reported contents for one year ((h)(1)); no monitoring is required ((f)). The operator cannot read mail, so actual knowledge would come from a report or a request. Penalties for a knowing and willful failure to report reach $600,000 for a first violation by a provider of this size ((e)). [Source](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2258A).
- **Sanctions (OFAC).** Read on 2026-11-12. "All U.S. persons must comply with OFAC sanctions, including all U.S. citizens and permanent residents regardless of where they are located" ([FAQ 11](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/1501)), whoever is invited and wherever they live. OFAC's enforcement guidelines define an apparent violation as conduct "that constitutes an actual or possible violation", and treat knowledge as a factor in the response, not as a condition ([31 C.F.R. part 501, app. A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/appendix-A_to_part_501)).
  - *Places.* FAQ 10 (same page) names the jurisdictions under "comprehensive sanctions": Cuba, Iran, North Korea, "the Crimea region of Ukraine" and "the so-called Donetsk People's Republic and Luhansk People's Republic regions of Ukraine", and blocks the governments of Cuba, Iran, North Korea and Venezuela. Syria is not on it, and OFAC's [list of programs](https://ofac.treasury.gov/sanctions-programs-and-country-information) has no Syria program, only one about Assad (PAARSS). Everything else, Russia and Belarus included, works by lists: the [Sanctions List Search](https://sanctionssearch.ofac.treas.gov/) covers the SDN List and the other lists. The places change; the terms name none, and the procedure reads FAQ 10 on the day.
  - *Iran.* [31 CFR 560.540](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/560.540)(a)(1) authorises the export "to Iran of services incident to the exchange of communications over the internet", with no condition on price; OFAC FAQ 338 says these include cloud-based services and lists "instant messaging, chat and email" ([Iran FAQs](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/1551)). It does not authorise services "intended for the Government of Iran" except those "publicly available at no cost to the user" ((a)(6), (b)(1)), which an invitation-only service is not, nor for "any person blocked pursuant to this part". FAQ 1088: a cloud provider uses what it learns "in the ordinary course of business" to confirm the customer is not a blocked person; OFAC "does not generally expect" it to evaluate the end use.
  - *Cuba.* [31 CFR 515.578](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/515.578)(a)(1)(i) authorises "Services incident to the exchange of communications over the internet, such as instant messaging, chat and email"; (b)(1) excludes services intended for "a prohibited official of the Government of Cuba" or "a prohibited member of the Cuban Communist Party", and organisations they control, beyond services "widely available to the public at no cost to the user" ((a)(4)). Last amended 2026-09-30 (91 FR 61748).
  - *Crimea, Donetsk and Luhansk.* OFAC's [Ukraine-/Russia-related page](https://ofac.treasury.gov/sanctions-programs-and-country-information/ukraine-russia-related-sanctions) lists General License 22, "Authorizing the Exportation of Certain Services and Software Incident to Internet-Based Communications", not marked expired; the page does not say which region it covers, and a search of OFAC's site ties it to E.O. 14065, the Donetsk and Luhansk regions. For Crimea, the same search found an OFAC fact sheet describing General License 9 for "services incident to the exchange of personal communications over the internet", codified in 31 CFR part 589. Neither licence's current text was opened (eCFR refused automated reads): read both before relying on them.
  - *North Korea.* No general licence for internet services was found on OFAC's North Korea FAQs; the procedure invites nobody there.
  - *Other people's terms.* Microsoft's API Terms 3(b)(17)(3) forbid access "from an embargoed country", whatever OFAC allows; the terms of AWS, OpenAI, Google, GitHub and Meta for those places were not read.
  - *So:* the terms make eligibility depend on sanctions not forbidding the service, in general words, and allow blocking; the invitation procedure applies the rule, and an IP block is not compliance by itself. A person on a list can live anywhere, the United States included.

Enforced as an unfair practice by the Department, with no private cause of action ((9), (10)). [Source](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/501.171).
- **California.**
  - *CCPA/CPRA* applies to "for-profit businesses" that do business in California and meet one of: gross annual revenue of $26.625 million or more; buying, selling or sharing the personal information of 100,000 or more California residents or households; or half their revenue from selling or sharing it ([CPPA FAQ](https://cppa.ca.gov/faq.html), [threshold](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/cpi_adjustment.html)). The facts today: revenue $0, nothing sold or shared, far fewer than 100,000 people, and no for-profit entity. Not a "business"; revisit before paid plans.
  - *CalOPPA* (Bus. & Prof. Code 22575) is a separate test with no size threshold: an operator of a commercial website or online service that collects personally identifiable information from California consumers must post a policy that identifies the categories collected and the third parties they may go to, describes how a consumer reviews and asks to change their data, how material changes are told, its effective date, how "do not track" signals are handled, and whether other parties collect data about the user's activity across sites. Whether a free, invitation-only beta is "commercial" is unclear; the privacy page meets each item anyway (effective date, categories, third parties, review in Settings, changes by mail, Do Not Track, no cross-site collection). The official text could not be opened (leginfo refused automated access); read on [FindLaw](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/business-and-professions-code/bpc-sect-22575/).
- **COPPA** applies to a service "directed to children" or to an operator with "actual knowledge that it is collecting personal information from a child" ([15 U.S.C. 6502](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/6502)). Accounts are for 18 and over. Adults' mail will mention children; that is not collection from a child, and the privacy page now separates who may hold an account from who is mentioned in mail.
- **CAN-SPAM.** See "No postal address". [FTC guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business).

### Infrastructure facts the pages rely on

- **DynamoDB TTL** deletes expired items "typically within a few days after their expiration", so expiry is not deletion on the day ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazondynamodb/latest/developerguide/howitworks-ttl.html)). Reads filter expired items in the code.
- **Point-in-time recovery** keeps "up to 35 days of recovery points", 35 by default and configurable from 1 to 35 ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazondynamodb/latest/developerguide/Point-in-time-recovery.html)); enabled on `permaily-mail` (`deploy/tofu/mail.tf`), at the default.
- **S3 Object Lock, compliance mode:** a locked version "can't be overwritten or deleted by any user, including the root user", and "after the retention period expires, the object version can be overwritten or deleted": it is not deleted by the lock's end ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/object-lock.html)). `deploy/tofu/anchors.tf` has no lifecycle rule for production, and the enclave locks each commit for `anchor_retention_days` (3650). A commit holds the record's name (for accounts `account-<id>`), version, digest and time (`internal/store/s3commits.go`), so it can be matched to an account by its identifier.
- **Amplify access logs:** "Amplify retains all access logs for an app until you delete the app", available in the console two weeks per request ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amplify/latest/userguide/using-access-logs.html)); AWS analyses them as CloudFront logs, whose fields include the viewer's IP address and User-Agent ([CloudFront log fields](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudFront/latest/DeveloperGuide/standard-logs-reference.html)). The site is on Amplify with CloudFront (`deploy/tofu/site.tf`).
- **The service's own logs** name no address or account (threat model, "Logging"); no code in the enclave or the host logs a client's IP address (searched 2026-11-12). The host is untrusted and could be changed to log it.
- **Cookies** (`internal/httpapi/accounts.go`, `internal/domain/accounts.go`): the session cookie's `Max-Age` is 30 days while the server ends a session after 7 days without use; the provider cookie is 10 minutes, or 20 while an address is confirmed (`ProviderConfirmLifetime`).
- **What a provider gives** (`internal/idp`): scopes `openid email profile` (Google, Microsoft), `read:user user:email` (GitHub), `email public_profile` (Meta); read are the subject, an address, whether it is verified and a name. Kept: provider and subject (`providerDoc`). A vouched address becomes the contact address on sign-up (`firstTime`); an unvouched one waits in memory to be offered in the form; the name is not used. Unlinking (`UnlinkProvider`) leaves the contact address.
- **Notices** (`internal/services/accounts/notices.go`): one try, dropped after `noticeWait` or when 32 are in flight; none for "sign devices out" (`closeAccountSessions`); made and removed admins are told (`admins.go`).

### The license and the CLA

- **"Source-available", not "open source"**, as before: the license restricts fields of use.
- **Compared** (corrected). [PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0](https://polyformproject.org/licenses/noncommercial/1.0.0) allows any noncommercial purpose, lists personal uses ("research, experiment, and testing for the benefit of public knowledge, personal study", hobby projects and the like), allows charities, schools and public research organisations "regardless of the source of funding", and grants distribution and "changes and new works" for permitted purposes: wider than the Personal Use License, which forbids distribution and organisations altogether. [MPL-2.0](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/MPL/2.0/) is not permissive: it is a file-level copyleft, whose covered files must stay available in source form when distributed in executable form (3.2), while a larger work may be under other terms (3.3). It is in `third-party-notices.md` for `lightningcss`, a build tool of the site and the web app whose own files are not distributed, so it asks nothing today; it would if they were.
- **GPL-2.0.** Distributing the kernel and `nsm.ko` as object code in `permaily.eif` requires the source with it (3(a)), or a written offer valid "for at least three years, to give any third party" a copy (3(b)); passing on AWS's offer is allowed "only for noncommercial distribution" and only for an offer received (3(c)) ([GPL-2.0](https://opensource.org/license/gpl-2-0)). Since ADR 0023 Permaily does not distribute the image; the releases published before it do (engineering item 3).
- **License 1.2.** 1.1 defined "You" as a natural person acting for no entity, so an auditor working for a firm, or a journalist for a newspaper, had no license to inspect or verify, against "anyone can verify" on the site; and GitHub's terms (D.5) let any user view and fork a public repository on GitHub whatever the license says. 1.2 grants Inspect and Verify, building included, to any entity and anyone acting for one, only to inspect, verify and publish what they find, under the same restrictions; and section 3.4 allows forks made through the hosting service's own features where its terms require it, with no further rights. Running the code stays for natural persons, for themselves.
- **CLA 1.2.** Fixes three of the review's points in the text: the agreement now reaches only pull requests from the signature on (1.1's "by submitting" reached issues and anything unsigned); material that is not the contributor's own work is outside the grant, stays under its own license, and must be declared; and it no longer claims the bot records the version signed. Since 2026-11-12 the workflow links the text at a fixed commit, and each version's signatures go to their own file (`signatures/cla-1.2.json`), so what is recorded says which version was signed; the bot itself records no version field. An electronic signature "may not be denied legal effect ... solely because it is in electronic form" ([15 U.S.C. 7001](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/7001)).
- **Trademark.** Unregistered rights come from use, so the owner is whoever uses the mark for the service, not automatically the copyright holders; the policy no longer says otherwise (open question 2). The domain rule now covers only confusing names: a blanket ban on any domain containing the name would reach good-faith commentary, which the law allows.

### The terms of service

- **Liability.** Data loss is not necessarily "consequential" loss, so it is no longer listed as an example of excluded damage; the cap of $100 stands for what the contract governs, and binds only someone who accepted the terms (recorded since engineering item 1).
