# 0017 Accounts, sign-in and how account data is kept

Status: Accepted. Sign-up by invitation, sign-in by email, sessions, outgoing mail, passkeys, "passkey only" and recovery codes are built (`internal/services/accounts`, `internal/sesmail`, `internal/webauthn`). Settings, deleting an account, the operator's tool and signing in with Google, GitHub, Meta and Microsoft too; the providers have not been tried against the real ones yet, which needs an application registered at each. What the operator can do to an account, changing the contact address and the notices by mail are built too (2026-11-12), and not released yet. Confirming again before a sensitive change, and admins in the web app, are ADR 0022. Accounts are ready for two enclaves at once behind one address (2026-11-12, "Two enclaves at once"), and not released yet.
Date: 2026-11-11 (decided on 2026-11-09)

## Context

F1 has one test mailbox behind a token. F2 needs people: accounts, ways to sign in, sessions, and a rule for what the operator may see of them. The operator must be able to help a user (see who is registered and their contact address), and must not be able to take an account over by writing to the table.

## Decision

**Ways to sign in.** Passkeys (relying party `app.permaily.com`), email (one message with a link and a six-digit code), Google, GitHub, Meta and Microsoft. A provider account is linked to an existing account on its own when the provider says the address is verified and it is the account's. Signing in with ChatGPT is left out for now.

**Contact address.** Every account has one, verified. Recovery goes through it. An account can switch to "passkey only": email and every provider are turned off for sign-in, and recovery codes are issued instead.

**Sign-up by invitation.** Single-use codes, made by an operator with a command-line tool and the admin token, of which the image holds only the hash.

**Sessions.** Seven days without use, thirty days at most, listed and closed per device. Cookies carry the `__Host-` prefix, so the public site at the apex cannot read or overwrite them.

**No second check for sensitive actions.** Changing how one signs in, changing the contact address, approving a connection or deleting the account asks for nothing beyond the session. Accepted risk: a stolen session can do all of it until it ends. The person is told by mail of each (see "Notices by mail"). Replaced on 2026-11-12 by ADR 0022: these changes need a proof of who the person is from the last ten minutes.

**Deleting an account.** Its connections are revoked at once and the account is locked; after seven days in which it can be cancelled, its keys are destroyed.

**Four ways to keep a piece of data.**

| Treatment | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sealed (encrypted, only an enclave of the deployment reads it) | Sign-ins in progress (with the hashes of their codes, which a million tries offline would find), bodies, attachments, raw mail, sender, recipients, subject, snippet, attachment names, Gmail confirmation codes, the text of the user's rules, the approved query of each connection, receipts, connection tokens, webhook and provider secrets | The operator must never see it |
| Readable and signed (plain text with a code only the enclave can make) | Contact address, name, language, account state, dates, ways to sign in (kind, passkey public key, provider and subject), mailbox (forwarding address, state, retention), connection (vendor, state, dates, schedule) | The operator sees it to give support, and cannot change it: an address written into the table by hand would be a way into the account |
| Hash only | Sessions, invitations, recovery codes | Whoever reads the table cannot use them |
| Plain | Identifiers, sizes, expiry times, counters | Needed to index and expire |

The public promise becomes: we see your account data, never your mail.

**Changes to account data go through the enclave** (the operator's tool), never by hand in the table: what is signed would stop verifying.

**What the operator may do to an account** (decided by the maintainer on 2026-11-11). Operators manage accounts through the enclave, almost everything a platform's admins manage: block and unblock one, ask to delete one and cancel that, sign its devices out, find one by its address, list and revoke invitations. The limit stays: nothing that lets the operator enter an account or read its mail. So the operator cannot change a contact address, add or remove a way to sign in, turn "passkey only" off, or sign in as someone. The consequence is accepted: someone who loses their mail, their passkeys and their recovery codes cannot be given the account back by support.

**Outgoing mail** (sign-in links and codes, verification, notices) goes through Amazon SES from a subdomain of its own, with the role the enclave already has. Sign-in codes pass through AWS, which is trusted already. By email the user is told only of security and access: a sign-in from a new device, a connection approved, a connection about to end, a change in how the account is signed into. Everything else is shown in the app.

**English only** in the first version, for the app and for the mail it sends.

## How it is built

Decided while building the first piece, each to keep whoever runs the storage out of accounts.

- **The account's record** is one document, readable and signed, read through `store.Anchored`. An address leads to its account through a pointer, and the pointer proves nothing: the record says which address is its own. A record that does not verify, or whose committed version is gone, is an error and never "no account", so a sign-up cannot take the address over.
- **A sign-in that was started is a sealed entry, and its count of wrong codes is in the enclave's memory** (until 2026-11-12 the whole of it was in memory; see "Two enclaves at once"). The count could otherwise be set back by putting an earlier copy of it in the table, and a six-digit code falls to whoever can guess without limit. A restart ends the sign-ins that were waiting, since an enclave ends none made before it started; the person asks for another message.
- **A code takes five tries, and an address ten wrong codes a day.** After that the address's messages carry only the link, which cannot be guessed, until a day passes: guessing stops and the person still signs in.
- **Starting a sign-in answers the same, and as fast, for every address.** The lookup and the message happen after the answer. An address without an account gets a message only with an invitation; an address is sent five messages an hour at most, and an invitation starts five sign-ups an hour at most.
- **Sessions and invitations are entries, signed with where they are stored** and kept by the hash of their secret, so one cannot be written by hand or copied into another account. How long one lasts is read from what was signed.
- **The cookie** is `__Host-permaily-session`, `Secure`, `HttpOnly`, `SameSite=Strict`. A request that changes something must also come from the app's own origin (`Origin`, `Sec-Fetch-Site`).

- **The link asks before it signs in.** A link can be sent on by whoever asked for it, to sign another person into the asker's account. The page that opens it asks whose sign-in it is (`POST /api/v1/auth/email/look`, which ends nothing and is answered only with the link's token), shows the address and waits for the person. The code needs none of this: it is typed in the browser that asked.
- **Outgoing mail** is from `no-reply@mail.permaily.com` (`mail_from` in `deploy/enclave.json`): a domain used for nothing else, signed with DKIM by SES, with DMARC set to refuse what fails. Nothing is received there. An image without `mail_from` has no accounts. The administrator of the AWS account could make SES keep a copy of what is sent, so the enclave reads those settings and tells (ADR 0021).

### Passkeys, "passkey only" and recovery codes

- **WebAuthn is checked by this repository's own code** (`internal/webauthn`): what a browser answers when a passkey is made and when it is used, for ES256, EdDSA and RS256 keys. No attestation is asked for (`attestation: "none"`) and none is trusted: which device holds the key does not matter here. A library would bring TPM, JWT and metadata code into the image; the checks are compared with a known library in `tests/webauthn-differential`, the way `pkg/provenance` is.
- **Checked against what real devices answer.** The checks are strict (a key holds exactly the fields of its algorithm, no CBOR tags, client data with no member twice), so they are run on 96 public captures of real authenticators and browsers (`internal/webauthn/testdata/vectors`): none is refused for a reason other than the ones meant (no user verification, an algorithm not offered, a use that carries a credential). No public capture was found for several password managers, for a sign-in from another device, or for current Safari and Firefox as such: the first sign that one of them is refused would come from a person.
- **The relying party** is the app's host (`app.permaily.com`) and the only origin accepted is the app's. Passkeys are discoverable (`residentKey: required`) and need the person (`userVerification: required`). The user handle is the account's identifier, so the answer to a sign-in says which account it is for and no address is asked: signing in with a passkey tells nobody who has an account.
- **A ceremony is a sealed entry**, like a sign-in by mail: a challenge of 32 random bytes, five minutes, once, ended through any enclave that started before it was made. A restart ends the ones that were waiting.
- **Passkeys are part of the account's record** (signed, anchored): the credential's identifier, its public key as the authenticator gave it, a name, when it was made and whether it is synced. So one written into the table by hand is not a way in, and one that was removed does not come back. Twenty at most.
- **The signature counter is not kept.** Synced passkeys always say zero, and keeping it would write a locked commit for every sign-in. Accepted: a copied authenticator is not noticed by its counter.
- **"Passkey only"** needs a passkey, and from then on a sign-in by mail for that address sends nothing (the answer stays the same) and one that was already started is refused when it ends. Turning it on makes ten recovery codes, shown once and kept by their hash in the account's record; each works once, with the account's address, and is taken out of the record when used, so it does not come back either. Five wrong recovery codes an hour for an address, counted in memory. Turning the mode off, or asking for new codes, drops the old ones.
- **Removing a passkey** is refused for the last one while "passkey only" is on: the account would have no way in but recovery codes.
- **Every failure of a passkey ceremony is the same error** (`invalid_passkey`), and of a recovery the same as a wrong code (`invalid_code`): none says which part was wrong.
- **A recovery code** is sixteen characters of the invitation alphabet, shown in four groups; its hash is taken with the account's identifier. Wrong ones are counted only for an address whose account has "passkey only" on, so made-up addresses do not fill the memory, and past the limit the right code is refused too with the same error, never with one that would say such an account exists. An address without an account costs the storage the same reads as one with.
- **Anyone can start a ceremony**, so starting one is never refused. Each is a write to the table that ends in five minutes (until 2026-11-12 they were in memory, ten thousand at most, the oldest dropped).
- **Changes to an account's record never undo one another**, in one enclave or in two. Each is a read, an edit and a write, and the write is refused when anything was committed for the record since the read (`store.Anchored.PutRecordAt`, ADR 0016). Then the record is read again and the edit runs again on what is there, four times at most; after that the change fails with an error and nothing is written. A change that raced with the account's removal is refused the same way, so the account does not come back. Inside one enclave the changes still go one at a time (`change`), so that its own requests do not make each other start over. What else two enclaves at once would break is listed below.
- **Not done on purpose:** the synced flag of a passkey is not read again at each sign-in, turning "passkey only" on closes no session, and a recovery leaves the mode on.

The web app's API for this, all under `/api/v1/`, JSON, binary values as base64url without padding in the shapes of `PublicKeyCredential.toJSON()`:

| Request | Needs | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| `POST auth/passkey/start` | nothing | `{ceremony, options}`: the options of `navigator.credentials.get` |
| `POST auth/passkey/finish` `{ceremony, credential}` | nothing | the account and its session, with the cookie, as signing in by mail does |
| `POST auth/recovery` `{address, code}` | nothing | the same |
| `GET passkeys` | a session | `{passkeys: [{id, name, created_at, synced}], passkey_only, recovery_codes_left}` |
| `POST passkeys/start` | a session | `{ceremony, options}`: the options of `navigator.credentials.create` |
| `POST passkeys/finish` `{ceremony, name, credential}` | a session | the passkey made |
| `PATCH passkeys/{id}` `{name}` | a session | the passkey |
| `DELETE passkeys/{id}` | a session | nothing (204) |
| `POST account/passkey-only` `{on}` | a session | `{passkey_only, recovery_codes}`: the codes when it was turned on |
| `POST account/recovery-codes` | a session, "passkey only" on | `{recovery_codes}`: ten new ones |

### Settings, deleting an account and the operator's tool

- **The name** is changed by the person (`PATCH account {name}`), one printable line of at most eighty characters. The contact address is changed another way, with a code sent to the new one (see "Changing the contact address").
- **Deleting an account takes seven days.** Asking for it (`POST account/delete`) sets the account's state to `closing` with the day it ends, in the anchored record, and closes every other session. Until that day the person can still sign in, sees only that the account is closing, and can cancel (`POST account/delete/cancel`); every other change is refused (`account_closing`). Connections, when they exist, are revoked the moment it is asked.
- **What deleting removes.** When the day comes the enclave removes the sessions, the pointer from the address, the entry in the list of accounts and the record itself, and commits that the record is gone, so an earlier copy put back in the table does not bring the account back, and the address can sign up again with an invitation. An account that is due is also removed the moment anyone tries to use it, so taking its entry out of the list of what is closing does not keep it alive.
- **Only the newest version of an account's record is kept.** `store.Anchored` writes each version under its own name; for accounts the one before is deleted once the new one is committed, so a name or a passkey that was removed does not stay in the table. A version that failed to be deleted is harmless (it is not the committed one) and is removed with the account.
- **Lists are entries that prove nothing.** The list of accounts (for the operator's tool) and the list of accounts that are closing (for the enclave to find the ones that are due) are entries written next to the record. What is shown or acted on is always the signed, anchored record they point to: an entry written by hand lists nothing and deletes nothing.
- **The order of a removal**: the pointer from the address (only if it still leads to this account), the entry in the list of accounts, the sessions, the record (the commit that it is gone, then every stored version), and last the entry in the list of what is closing. The record goes after everything that is found through it, and while it stands it says the account is due, so nothing uses it and every use or pass of the loop starts again from the first step. A failure in the middle leaves an account nobody can use, never one half usable.
- **Details.** The day is seven times twenty-four hours from the request, to the second. Asking again keeps the day. A recovery code works while closing: it may be the only way in to cancel. An empty name is allowed. The loop runs every ten minutes. Every request with a session reads the account's record, so a session of an account that is due, or one put back after the account was removed, does nothing; it costs one anchored read a request, which a short memory of records would save if it ever matters.
- **What the operator's tool shows** (`GET /v1/admin/accounts`, `GET /v1/admin/accounts/{id}`, with the admin token; `scripts/accounts.sh`): the identifier, the address, the name, the state, dates, how many passkeys and sessions, whether "passkey only" is on, which providers are linked (never the subject at each), whether it is blocked and who asked to delete it. It is what the enclave vouches for, where the table is only what is stored. What it changes is in "What the operator can do to an account".

### Google, GitHub, Meta and Microsoft

- **The flow** is OAuth's authorization code with PKCE (S256), a state and, where the provider signs who the person is, a nonce. Starting (`POST auth/provider/start {provider, invitation}`) answers the provider's address to go to and sets a cookie for this browser alone (`__Host-permaily-provider`, `HttpOnly`, `Secure`, `SameSite=Lax`, ten minutes); what was started is a sealed entry found by the hash of the state, once, for ten minutes. The provider sends the browser back to `GET /api/v1/auth/provider/callback`, which ends it only for the browser that started it (the cookie): an answer of the provider carried to another person's browser signs nobody in, which is the same hole the sign-in link had.
- **Who the person is** comes from the provider over TLS that ends in the enclave. Google and Microsoft sign it (an ID token, RS256, checked by this repository's own code against the keys the provider publishes: issuer, audience, expiry, nonce); GitHub and Meta are asked with the access token just received (`/user` and `/user/emails`; `/me`). The access token is used for that and thrown away: nothing of the provider is kept but which provider and which subject.
- **A provider account leads to an account by its subject**, never by its address: the pointer `login#<provider>#<subject>` proves nothing, and the account's record lists the provider and the subject.
- **The first time**, the provider's address decides. If it is verified and an account has it, the provider is linked to that account (decided on 2026-11-09). If it is verified and no account has it, an account is made, with an invitation as for any sign-up. If it is not verified, the provider proved who the person is there and not that the address is theirs: the address is confirmed by a message with a code, as signing in by mail does, before anything is linked or made.
- **What counts as verified.** Google: `email_verified`. GitHub: the primary address, when GitHub says it is verified. Microsoft: only when the token says the owner of the address's domain was verified (`xms_edov`) or the account is a personal one; a work or school account's `email` is whatever its administrator typed, and trusting it has let people take over accounts elsewhere. Meta: never, since it says nothing of whether an address is verified; a Meta sign-in always confirms the address by mail the first time.
- **"Passkey only" turns providers off** for signing in, as it does mail. A provider can be unlinked in Settings unless it is the only way left to sign in.
- **The providers' secrets** (each application's client secret) are given to the enclave by the operator over the attested TLS connection (`PUT /v1/admin/providers/{provider}`, with the admin token) and kept sealed under the root secret, like the TLS key. They are not in the image, where they would be public. A provider with no secret given is off, and the app does not offer it (`GET auth/providers`).
- **Details.** One account of each provider for an account. Linking from Settings is a request of its own that remembers the session's account, since the session cookie is `Strict` and does not travel with the provider's answer; a provider account whose vouched address is another account's is refused there. Confirming an address takes the code only: the link of that message signs in by mail and links nothing. An account that is closing is not signed in by a provider's first time. Removing an application's secret turns the provider off and keeps the links. After the answer the browser is sent only to a path of the app. A failure reaches the app as one of seven fixed reasons, of which the one a stranger can cause (`provider_failed`) covers every cause they could learn from.
- **The callback names the provider of a trip that failed** (2026-11-11), next to the reason: `#provider_error=<reason>&provider=<id>`, so the screen can say "Signing in with Google didn't work" instead of "that provider". It is only ever one of the four fixed identifiers (`internal/httpapi` checks it against `idp.Providers` before writing it), and it is the one this browser asked for when it started the trip, so it tells nobody anything they did not choose themselves. When the enclave knows no such trip any more (the wait was too long, the answer was tried twice, or it arrived in another browser) it names none. A trip that worked names none either. The app takes only those four and falls back to the words without a name (`web/src/lib/arrival.ts`).
- **Meta is called Facebook on screen** (2026-11-11): the trip goes to Facebook's sign-in, and Meta's rules name the action "Continue with Facebook". The identifier stays `meta` in the API, the storage, the code and these documents; the web app maps it to the name in one place (`providerNames` in `web/src/lib/providers.ts`).
- **Not confirmed until tried against the real providers** (it needs an application registered at each): that Meta takes PKCE together with the client secret, a POST to its token address, and the proof of the secret together with a bearer token; the real shape of Microsoft's `xms_edov`; that GitHub takes the verifier alongside the secret. Meta's Graph API version is part of the address (`v27.0`) and has to be raised before Meta retires it.
- **Accepted.** Whoever writes to the storage can put back an old pointer from a subject to an account, which a signature made valid once. It lets nobody into an account they could not enter already: the record no longer lists the provider, so that provider account is asked to confirm again; and if its former owner links it again, it stays listed by two records.
- **What it rests on.** Each provider is trusted for the accounts linked to it: one that lies about who signed in, or (Google, GitHub, Microsoft) about an address being verified, takes those accounts. "Passkey only" is the way out. The operator, who registered the applications, knows the client secrets; with them nobody can make a signed token or an answer of the provider, which reach the enclave over TLS that the host cannot open.

Accepted: sessions are signed and not anchored. A session that was closed and is put back in the table by whoever runs the storage works again for whoever still holds its cookie, until its thirty days end. Anchoring each session would write a locked commit for every sign-in.

### What the operator can do to an account

Built on 2026-11-12, after the decision above. All with the admin token, on the API host, each `POST` without a body and answering the account's record after it (`AccountRecord`).

| Request | Does |
|---|---|
| `GET /v1/admin/accounts?address=<address>` | The account of exactly that address, read as a sign-in reads it, as a list of none or one |
| `POST /v1/admin/accounts/{id}/block`, `/unblock` | Blocks the account, or lets its person in again |
| `POST /v1/admin/accounts/{id}/delete`, `/delete/cancel` | Asks to delete the account, or cancels a deletion the operator asked for |
| `POST /v1/admin/accounts/{id}/sessions/close` | Signs every device out |
| `GET /v1/admin/invitations` | The invitations not used yet: `{invitations: [{id, created_at, expires_at}]}` |
| `DELETE /v1/admin/invitations/{id}` | Revokes one (204) |

- **The limit is in the code twice.** There is no route that changes an address or a way to sign in, turns "passkey only" off or signs someone in. And the handlers of the operator's routes are given a service with eleven methods and nothing else (`httpapi.OperatorService`), each taking only an identifier, an address to look up, a place in the list or a count: a handler could not do it if someone added the route. A test names the eleven; adding one there is adding a way into every account.
- **A blocked account.** Its record says so with the time (`blocked_at`), every session of it is closed, and nobody signs in to it by any way: mail, passkey, provider, the code that confirms an address for a provider, recovery code. A session that is still in the table, or is put back, stands for nothing: every request reads the record. Blocking is apart from the state: a closing account can be blocked and a blocked one can be closing, and it is removed on its day all the same.
- **What a blocked person is told.** A sign-in that proved who it is and ends at a blocked account answers `account_blocked` (403, "this account is blocked; write to support@permaily.com") and signs nobody in; from the provider's callback it is the reason `account_blocked` in the fragment. Never before the proof: starting a sign-in by mail for a blocked address sends the message as for any other, a wrong code is a wrong code, and a passkey that is not the account's is a wrong passkey. So nobody learns from the outside that an address has an account, or that it is blocked. A right recovery code is told and not used up. Nothing is linked to a blocked account.
- **Deleting, asked by the operator**, is the same seven days and the same removal as when the person asks. The record says who asked (`closing_by`: `person` or `operator`), and the person sees it. Asking for an account that is closing already keeps its day and who asked first. The operator's request signs no device out: the person sees the account closing and can cancel it, also when the operator asked. A blocked person cannot, because they are not signed in. The operator cancels only a deletion the operator asked for (decided by the maintainer on 2026-11-12): one the person asked for answers 409 `not_cancellable` ("only the person can cancel a deletion they asked for"), changes nothing and is not counted. To keep the account of a blocked person who asked to delete it, the operator unblocks it and the person cancels.
- **Finding an account by its address** reads the pointer and the signed record, and only looks: an account that is due is shown and not removed. An address is normalised as a sign-in normalises it, and it is exactly that address or nothing.
- **Invitations are listed under identifiers of their own.** An identifier is random (`inv_…`) and is stored next to the invitation and in an entry of a list; the code is in neither, and only its hash names where the invitation is kept. The list proves nothing: what is shown is the invitation an entry leads to, if it is there, verifies and has not ended. A used invitation was deleted when it was used, so it is not listed; neither is one that was revoked, which is deleted the same way. A sign-up that was started with an invitation that is then revoked ends with `invalid_invitation`. The invitations made before this (v0.10.0 and earlier) have no identifier: they are not listed and cannot be revoked, and end by themselves in thirty days. The list shows a thousand at most and says when there are more.
- **What the operator does is visible.** The enclave counts each kind of action it took for the operator (block, unblock, delete, cancel a deletion, sign devices out, invitations made, an invitation revoked, a provider's application set or removed) and serves the counts on `/status` under `admin`, with the time it started counting. Kinds and counts only: never an account, an address or an identifier. `permaily-verify` prints it and fails on nothing. Each action is logged too, by its kind alone. An action is counted when the enclave did it, also when it changed nothing (blocking an account that is blocked); what was refused is not counted. Invitations are counted one by one.
- **Not done on purpose.** The operator cannot change a name, a language or a session one by one, and is not told who a person is beyond what the record says. Closing an account's sessions sends its person no message. The operator's token has no levels: whoever holds it can do all of this.

### Changing the contact address

| Request | Needs | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| `POST account/address/start` `{address}` | a session | `{attempt, expires_in}`, as starting a sign-in by mail does |
| `POST account/address/finish` `{attempt, code}` | a session | the account, with its new address |

- **A code is sent to the new address, and proves it.** The message carries six digits and no link: the code is typed where the change was asked. It is the machinery of signing in by mail: the attempt is a sealed entry for ten minutes, takes five tries in each enclave and works once.
- **The attempt belongs to the account that started it**, and ends through any enclave. It signs nobody in, confirms no address for a provider and changes no other account's address; and a sign-in that was started changes no address. Tried as something else it is no attempt at all, and stays as it was.
- **Starting answers the same for every address**, and the message is sent whether the address is another account's or nobody's. That it is another account's is said only when the change ends (`address_in_use`), to whoever typed the code that was sent there. The account's own address is refused at once (`invalid_address`): it tells the asker nothing.
- **Limits.** An account asks five times an hour (`too_many_requests`). An address is sent five messages an hour, counted together with sign-in messages, and one over that is dropped without a word. An address that took ten wrong codes today is sent no code until a day passes: here there is no link to fall back on, and a six-digit code must not be guessed without limit by someone who wants an address that is not theirs.
- **How the account moves.** The pointer from an address is one entry, written only where none is or in the place of the one that was read: it is what keeps two accounts from having one address. The move takes four steps. (1) The record says the account is moving to the new address (`moving_to`), with a name for this move. (2) The pointer from the new address is written, to lead to the account. (3) The record says the new address is the account's, with `address_verified_at` now. (4) The old pointer is deleted, as it was read. The record names the address before the pointer is written, as a sign-up writes its record first, because a pointer that leads to a record which does not bear it out is taken for one that leads nowhere and is replaced by the next sign-up. The pointer is written again even when it leads to the account already, so that whoever read it a moment earlier as leading nowhere does not write over it.
- **A failure in between** leaves an account that both addresses find (after step 2), or one that only the old address finds: never one that cannot be found, and never an address that finds two accounts. A move that stops is taken back when it can be; when it cannot, the account stays found by both addresses, both of which its person proved, until the change is asked again, which ends it. The entry in the list of accounts does not change: it is kept by the time the account was made.
- **What else changes.** Every device stays signed in. Mail signs in at the new address and no longer at the old one, which is free: with an invitation it makes another account. Both addresses are told by mail.
- **A fresh proof is asked before the code is sent** (ADR 0022, 2026-11-12): a sign-in or a confirmation from the last ten minutes, by passkey or by a code at the current address. Until then, whoever held a session for a few minutes, and read the mail of an address of their own, took the account's address. A stolen session that is still fresh can still do it; the old address is told, and can only write to support, who can block the account and cannot give it back.

### Notices by mail

"By email the user is told only of security and access." Built here are the notices that need only accounts; the ones about connections come with them.

- **Which.** A sign-in from a device that had not signed in to the account before, by whatever way. A change in how the account is signed into: a passkey added or removed, a provider linked or unlinked, "passkey only" turned on or off, recovery codes made anew, a recovery code used. The contact address changed, to the old address and to the new one. Deleting asked or cancelled, by the person or by the operator. The account blocked or unblocked by the operator. They cannot be turned off.
- **What one holds.** Plain text, in English, from the same sender and through the same `Mailer` as a sign-in message, so the watch of ADR 0021 applies and none leaves while SES would keep a copy. The subject is fixed words. There is no code, no link, no token and no identifier in it, and nothing a person typed: not the name of a passkey, not an address. A notice names the app's host (`app.permaily.com`) and `support@permaily.com`, and says when, and what to do if it was not them. The notice to the old address does not name the new one: whoever reads the old one's mail now is not told where the person went.
- **What the browser said of itself** is never repeated. Whoever signs in chooses those words, and a sentence with no link in it can still pass for a message from Permaily. The notice of a new device names the browser from fixed words instead, as the list of sessions does: "Firefox on Mac", the browser alone, the system alone, or "not recognised". The names and the order they are tried in are the same in `domain.DeviceName` and in the web app's `describeDevice`, and a test compares them.
- **A device is known by a cookie of its own**: `__Host-permaily-device`, 32 random bytes, `HttpOnly`, `Secure`, `SameSite=Lax` (it has to come along when a provider sends the browser back), kept a year and set again at each sign-in. It is set when a sign-in ends, stands for nothing and is never logged. The account keeps which devices signed in to it as entries next to its sessions, each named by a hash of the cookie's value made with the account's identifier, signed where it is stored: fifty at most, each for a year from its last sign-in. A browser without the cookie is a new device every time.
- **The sign-in that makes the account is not a new device**, and a provider that is linked by that same sign-in is not told as a change.
- **Whoever writes to the table can only cause one notice more or one less**: more by deleting a device's entry, less by putting back one that was dropped. An entry is no session and no way into anything.
- **A notice never fails what caused it.** It is sent in line, after the change is stored, and holds the answer for five seconds at most; then the sending is given up. One that could not be sent is logged by its kind alone. Thirty-two are sent at a time and one more is dropped, logged as that. Dev mode keeps them in `/dev/outbox` with the other mail.
- **Details.** A recovery code used from a new device sends two messages, one for each. Asking to delete an account that is closing, or blocking one that is blocked, sends nothing again. A message from Permaily proves nothing by itself (docs/threat-model.md): a notice tells, and the app shows.

### Two enclaves at once

One enclave runs today. Since 2026-11-12 accounts are ready for two at once behind one address, any request landing on either (decided by the maintainer on 2026-11-12); the TLS identity, the test mailbox and the root secret were made ready the same day (ADR 0024). Whoever runs the operator's side is not trusted with people's accounts: they can read and write the whole storage, restart an enclave and see its logs. Nothing below gives them a way into an account they did not have when sign-ins in progress lived in memory.

**The rule** (maintainer, 2026-11-12): everything a request may need on another enclave lives in the storage; memory holds only what can be lost (a cache) or counted apart by each enclave (a limit). How each kind of state is kept is in `internal/AGENTS.md`.

What holds:

- **An account's record**: no change undoes another, and none brings a removed account back (above).
- **An invitation is used once**: of two sign-ups with it, one deletes it.
- **Two sign-ups of one address make one account**: the pointer from the address is written only where none is, and the sign-up that loses removes its record.
- **A session** is closed once, and one that was just closed is not written back by a request that was using it.
- **Removing an account** can be done by both at once: every step can be done twice. The pointer from the address is deleted only as it was read, so not the pointer of an account the address made meanwhile.
- **Deleting asked again in the second it was cancelled** leaves the account in the list of closing accounts: the entry is written again, and the cancel deletes only the entry it read.
- **Devices signed in at the same time** can leave an account with a few more sessions than it keeps; the next sign-in brings it back.
- **A change of the contact address**: no two accounts end with one address and none ends where it cannot be found, whatever the order two enclaves do it in, with each other and with a sign-up of the same address (see "Changing the contact address").
- **Blocking**: every request reads the account's record, so a session made through one enclave in the moment another blocks the account stands for nothing.
- **The TLS identity** (ADR 0024): one key, one ACME account and one certificate for both enclaves. The one that asks the CA claims the order in the sealed identity, written only if nothing changed since it was read; the other waits and serves what it stores, within ten minutes, without a restart. The CA's check may land on either: the challenge is kept sealed in the storage while the order is open, and both answer it. Two enclaves never make more orders than one would (`TestTwoEnclavesStartingTogetherOrderOneCertificate`, `TestTheCAsCheckReachesTheEnclaveThatDidNotOrder`).
- **The test mailbox** (ADR 0024): made once, by a write that only creates, and its state written at the version read. Two enclaves that start together use one data key, and neither undoes the other's change (`TestTheMailboxMadeByTwoEnclavesAtOnceIsOne`, `TestTwoEnclavesChangingTheMailboxDoNotUndoEachOther`).
- **The root secret** (ADR 0024): its records are made by writes that only create. Two enclaves of one image that start together get one secret, and two that hand it over at once write the next release's record once.
- **Confirming again and admins** (ADR 0022): the time of a session's proof is in its signed entry, an admin's role in the account's record (changed only through `change`, read with the record), the admin log in entries. A session confirmed through one enclave changes through the other, and an admin made through one acts through the other (`TestASessionConfirmedThroughOneEnclaveChangesThroughTheOther`, `TestAnAdminMadeThroughOneEnclaveActsThroughTheOther`).
- **A sign-in that was started ends through either enclave** (2026-11-12): by mail (the code and the link), a change of the contact address, a confirmation by mail or with a passkey, a passkey ceremony (signing in, making a passkey), a trip to a provider, and a provider's sign-in that waits for its address. Each is an entry of its own (`pending#<kind>#<name>`, ten minutes, five for a ceremony, twenty for an address to confirm), sealed with a key from the root secret (`envelope.SealText`), bound to where it is stored: whoever reads the table sees that a sign-in of some kind is in progress and nothing else of it. Not the address, not a code or a hash of one (which a million tries offline would find), not a token, a challenge, a PKCE verifier, a state, a nonce or an invitation. One moved to another place does not open. What names it is its identifier, or, for a trip and an address waiting, the hash of the state or of the browser's cookie, both 32 random bytes. When it was made and when it ends are read from what was sealed (`pending.go`; `TestASignInByMailStartedThroughOneEnclaveEndsThroughTheOther`, `TestAPasskeyCeremonyStartedThroughOneEnclaveEndsThroughTheOther`, `TestATripToAProviderStartedThroughOneEnclaveEndsThroughTheOther`, `TestWhatTheTableShowsOfASignInInProgress`).
- **It works once.** Ending it writes a mark next to it, only where none is (a write at version 0, which DynamoDB makes conditional), and then deletes it as it was read. Of two enclaves that end it at once, one writes the mark; the other is told the code is wrong, or the passkey (`TestASignInInProgressEndsOnceThroughTwoEnclaves`). A copy put back after it was used is refused by every enclave while the mark is there, and the mark holds nothing. Each enclave also remembers, for as long as the sign-in could be ended, that it ended it: a copy put back with the mark deleted still ends nothing there. What that rests on: the condition of DynamoDB's write, and the enclave's memory. What is left: a copy put back with its mark deleted ends once more in each other enclave that did not see it used, within its ten minutes, for whoever holds its code or its link (`TestASignInPutBackAfterItWasUsedEndsNothing` shows it). That takes someone who writes to the table and reads the person's mail, who could ask for a message of their own, and still meets "passkey only", the block and the account's own state at that moment.
- **An enclave ends nothing that was started before it started** (`TestAnEnclaveEndsNoSignInMadeBeforeItStarted`). Its counts of wrong answers are in memory and a restart forgets them; this rule forgets the sign-ins with them, as a restart did when they lived in memory. So restarting an enclave, which the operator can do, gives nobody more tries. A deployment ends every sign-in in progress, as before: the person asks again. A sign-in made in the second an enclave starts, by an enclave whose clock is behind, may be refused by it: the person asks again.
- **Limits are counted in each enclave's memory, on purpose.** A counter in the storage could be put back by whoever writes to the table; one in memory cannot. With N enclaves each limit is at most N times what it says, accepted for two:

| Limit | One enclave | Two enclaves |
|---|---|---|
| Tries on one code (counted by the attempt, for as long as it lives, also after it was used; out of them in one enclave, it is deleted from the table) | 5 | 10: one in a hundred thousand for a six-digit code |
| Wrong codes a day for an address, before its messages carry only the link | 10 | 20: two in a hundred thousand a day |
| Messages an hour for an address (sign-in, confirmation, new address) | 5 | 10 |
| Sign-ups started an hour with an invitation | 5 | 10 |
| New addresses asked an hour by an account, and confirmations asked by mail | 5 | 10 |
| Wrong recovery codes an hour for an address | 5 | 10, against sixteen characters of a 31-letter alphabet |
| Messages being sent at once, and notices | 32 and 32 | 64 and 64 |
| Sign-ins in progress an enclave remembers (one more is refused as a wrong answer, never forgotten, which would give its tries back) | 100 000 | 100 000 each |

None of them is dangerous at twice its value, so none was lowered.

- **The providers' applications** are read with `GetRecordAt` and written with `PutRecordAt`: two enclaves that set two providers at once keep both, and one that keeps losing gives up after four tries. An enclave reads the record again when what it holds is a minute old (`appsFresh`), never on every request: a secret given through one is used through the other within a minute, without a restart (`TestTwoEnclavesSetTheApplicationsOfTwoProviders`).
- **One provider account linked to two accounts in the same moment** ends linked to one, and the other is told `provider_in_use` (`TestOneProviderAccountLinkedToTwoAccountsAtOnce`). A link takes three steps, as a change of address does: the record says the provider account is being linked (`linking`), then the pointer is written where none is or where it leads to an account that neither lists nor is linking it, then the record lists it if the pointer still leads there. An enclave that finds the pointer leading to an account finds the record holding the provider account already, and refuses. A link that stopped halfway holds the provider account for a minute (`linkingFor`), and its last step is taken only within half of that, so another enclave whose clock is less than thirty seconds ahead does not take the pointer of a link that then ends.
- **An unlink** writes the record first, and deletes the pointer only after, as it was read, and only if the record does not hold the provider account any more. A change that was run again, or refused the second time, deletes no pointer of a link that stayed (`TestAnUnlinkRunAgainLeavesThePointerOfALinkThatStayed`).
- **A removal against a cancel landing while it runs** (`TestACancelLandingWhileARemovalRuns`). Before anything of the account is destroyed, the record says it is being removed (`removing`), written at the version that was read: a cancel written since makes that write fail, and the enclave reads again and keeps the account. Once written nothing takes it back, and the account is due from then on: a cancel that comes after it removes the account too and is told the session stands for nothing, never that it is kept. So the answer the person got is what happens.
- **Signing the other devices out** when a deletion is asked, when the account is blocked and when the operator or an admin signs every device out (`TestNoSessionMadeBeforeASignOutSurvivesIt`). The record counts how many times every device was signed out (`sign_outs`) and which session the last of them kept (`kept_session`); a session carries the count as the record had it when its person proved who they are. It stands only while the two agree, or while it is the one kept, read with the record on each request as the record already is. A count rather than a time, so that the clocks of two enclaves do not matter. A device that signs in through the other enclave in that moment, or through the same one, gets a session that stands for nothing.
- **The stale pointer of an address replaced on sign-up** is deleted as it was read, value and version, and the new one is written only where none is (`TestAStalePointerReplacedOnSignUpIsNotAnotherAccounts`). Every pointer and list entry carries a value of its own (`write`), since versions start over when an entry is deleted and made again. The same for the pointer of an address taken by a change of address, and of a provider account.

What is one enclave's, and stays so:

- **What `/status` says** is the enclave's that answered: the history of the mail watch (ADR 0021), of the key policy and of the commits it has seen, the trust root it took, and the counts of what the operator and the admins did (`admin`), each since that enclave started. Whoever verifies a deployment with several enclaves has to reach each of them; nothing in it says how many there are. The admin log (ADR 0022) is in the storage, and both read and write it.
- **Two sign-ins of one new device at once** send two notices, and one account changing to two addresses at once ends at one of them and tells the other request to ask again.
- **A commit added by whoever writes to the bucket**, in the moment between two changes of one account, loses one of them. It is noticed and not prevented (ADR 0016).

## What it rests on, and what is left

- Anyone can start a passkey ceremony or a trip to a provider, and each is a write to the table that ends in minutes (DynamoDB deletes it within days of its end, and nobody reads it after). It costs writes, and keeps nobody out: a person's own start is never refused.

- The records whose going back would matter here (ways to sign in, the "passkey only" switch, account state) go through `store.Anchored` (ADR 0016), so an earlier copy of them is refused.
- A provider that lies about a verified address can take over the account with that address. The "passkey only" mode is the way out for whoever does not accept that.
- SES starts in a sandbox: production access has to be asked of AWS.
- Sign-in by mail rests on the person's mail provider, on AWS and, as far as ADR 0021 does not close it, on whoever administers the AWS account. "Passkey only" rests on none of them.
- Whoever holds the admin token can block every account and ask to delete every account: it keeps people out, and lets nobody in. A deletion takes seven days, is told by mail and is counted on `/status`; the count is one enclave's and a restart starts it again.
- Someone who loses their mail, their passkeys and their recovery codes has lost the account: nobody can give it back.
- A stolen session changes the contact address with a code sent to an address of the thief's, while its proof is fresh (ten minutes after a sign-in or a confirmation, ADR 0022). The old address is told, and nothing undoes it.
- To design while building: the exact wording of each message, with the designer.
